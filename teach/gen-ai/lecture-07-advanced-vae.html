1.
β-VAE and Posterior Collapse

Weighting the KL Term

$$ \mathcal{L}_\beta = \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}[\log p_\theta(x\mid z)] - \beta \, D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q_\phi(z\mid x)\,\|\,p(z)\big) $$
  • \( \beta = 1 \) recovers the standard VAE
  • \( \beta > 1 \) presses the posterior harder toward the prior
  • No longer a likelihood bound — a deliberate trade

Disentanglement

  • A factorized prior pushes latent dimensions toward independence
  • Ideally one dimension controls one factor: pose, colour, thickness
  • Cost: higher \( \beta \) means worse reconstruction

Posterior Collapse

$$ q_\phi(z \mid x) \approx p(z) \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \text{the latent carries no information about } x $$
  • The KL term reaches zero and the decoder ignores \( z \)
  • Happens when the decoder is powerful enough to model \( x \) alone
  • Especially common with autoregressive decoders

Mitigations

  • KL annealing: ramp \( \beta \) up from 0 during training
  • Free bits: allow a KL budget per dimension without penalty
  • Weaken the decoder so it genuinely needs the latent

2.
VQ-VAE

Why Discrete Latents?

  • Continuous latents are prone to collapse and over-smoothing
  • Discrete codes match the structure of language-model machinery
  • And they give us the tokenizer promised in Lecture 05

Vector Quantization

$$ z_q = e_k, \qquad k = \arg\min_j \| z_e(x) - e_j \|_2 $$
  • Encode to a continuous \( z_e(x) \), then snap to the nearest codebook entry
  • The codebook \( \{e_1,\ldots,e_K\} \) is learned
  • The output is an index — a discrete token

The Gradient Problem

\( \arg\min \) has zero gradient almost everywhere — the encoder would never learn.
  • Straight-through estimator: copy the decoder gradient past the quantiser
  • Treat quantization as the identity during the backward pass

The VQ-VAE Loss

$$ \mathcal{L} = \underbrace{\log p(x \mid z_q)}_{\text{reconstruction}} + \underbrace{\|\operatorname{sg}[z_e] - e\|^2}_{\text{codebook}} + \beta \underbrace{\|z_e - \operatorname{sg}[e]\|^2}_{\text{commitment}} $$
  • Codebook loss: move entries toward the encoder outputs
  • Commitment loss: stop the encoder drifting away from the codebook
  • \( \operatorname{sg}[\cdot] \) is the stop-gradient operator

Codebook Collapse

  • Only a fraction of entries ever get used
  • Fixes: EMA updates, codebook restarts, lower code dimension
  • Utilisation is the metric to watch when training a tokenizer

3.
Hierarchical Models & VQGAN

Hierarchical Latents

  • One latent level struggles to hold both global and local structure
  • VQ-VAE-2: a top level for layout, a bottom level for detail
  • Each level is modelled by its own prior

Learning the Prior

  • A trained VQ-VAE has a mismatched prior: uniform over codes
  • Fit a second model — a PixelCNN or Transformer — over the code grid
  • This is exactly the two-stage recipe from Lecture 05

VQGAN

  • Add a perceptual loss and a patch discriminator to stage 1
  • Much sharper reconstructions at a high compression rate
  • The tokenizer behind Transformer-based image generation

4.
The Bridge to Modern Generation

The Bridge, Stated Plainly

The same encoder–decoder idea feeds both halves of the modern stack.
  • VQ-VAE → discrete tokens → autoregressive Transformers (Lecture 05)
  • VAE → continuous latent → latent diffusion (Lecture 14)

Two Roads from One Idea

  • Discrete route: quantize, then model tokens with cross-entropy
  • Continuous route: keep a smooth latent, then run diffusion inside it
  • Both exist to avoid generating in raw pixel space

Representation as Infrastructure

  • These models are rarely the final generator any more
  • They are the compression layer everything else builds on
  • Good generation depends on a good representation

Summary

  • β-VAE trades reconstruction for disentanglement
  • Posterior collapse happens when the decoder ignores the latent
  • VQ-VAE discretises the latent with a learned codebook
  • The straight-through estimator makes quantization trainable
  • VQ-VAE feeds Transformers; the continuous VAE feeds latent diffusion
