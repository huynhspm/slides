1.
Generative Model

Definition

Generative model: a model that learns or represents a probability distribution over data, allowing us to generate new samples from that distribution.
  • It captures the patterns and structure of the data distribution of observed data points.
  • Generating new samples is a consequence of learning this underlying structure.

The Rise of GenAI

Generative modelling has existed for decades, so why did Generative AI only take off after 2020?
  • Classical (before 2014): GMMs, HMMs, graphical models, Boltzmann machines: solid theory, but small data and simple distributions
  • Deep generative models (2014–2019): VAE, GAN, PixelCNN, WaveNet: realistic samples, but mostly inside research labs
  • Turning point (2020): diffusion models (DDPM) beat GANs with stable training; GPT-3 shows the power of scaling
  • Boom (2022–2023): DALL·E 2, Stable Diffusion, ChatGPT: generation becomes a product used by millions
  • Agentic era (2024–): reasoning models and agents that plan, use tools, and write code: from generating content to taking actions

What Made GenAI Take Off?

The core ideas are old, the breakthrough came from scaling them:

🧠 Models 106 → 1011+ parameters

Transformers and diffusion models scale smoothly

Scaling laws: more parameters → better samples

×
🗂️ Data 1012 tokens · 109 images

Web-scale text corpora and image–text pairs (LAION-5B)

Self-supervised: no manual labels needed

×
⚡ Compute 1000s of GPUs · weeks

Large accelerator clusters with distributed training

Hardware + distributed training make it feasible

Beyond scale, accessibility mattered: open-source releases and user-friendly products turned research models into everyday tools.

2.
Discriminative vs Generative

The Prediction Problem

The most familiar task: look at an observation and predict its label.

Given an observation \( x \), predict its label \( y \):   \( x \xrightarrow{\;f(x)\;} y \)
Image → Cat / Dog
Text → Sentiment
Patient data → Diagnosis

Discriminative Modeling

\( \boxed{p_\theta(y \mid x)} \)
the probability of a label \( y \) given an observation \( x \)
🐱 Image \( x \)
→
Discriminative model
→
\( p(\text{cat} \mid x) = 0.92 \)
\( p(\text{dog} \mid x) = 0.08 \)
  • Learns the decision boundary between classes
  • Focuses only on the label \( y \), not the input distribution \( p(x) \)

Generative Modeling

Instead of asking “What is the label?”, ask “What does the data look like?”
Unconditional
\( \boxed{p_\theta(x)} \)
The data itself

What do plausible observations look like?

Joint
\( \boxed{p_\theta(x, y)} \)
Data and labels

How are observations and labels distributed together?

Conditional
\( \boxed{p_\theta(x \mid c)} \)
Data given \( c \)

What does the data look like given a condition?

\( p(x, y) = p(y \mid x)\, p(x) \): the joint contains both \( p(y \mid x) \) and \( p(x) \)

Conditional Generation

\( \boxed{\tilde{x} \sim p_\theta(x \mid c)} \)
generate a new sample \( \tilde{x} \) given a condition \( c \)
Class label
label → image
Text prompt
text → image
Sketch / map
sketch → image
Classifier:\( x \to y \)predict a label from the data Conditional generator:\( c \to x \)create controllable data from a condition

Classification vs Generation

The same dataset of cats and dogs, two different questions:

Discriminative
\( p(y \mid x) \)  “Is this a cat?”
Learns the boundary between classes
Generative
\( p(x \mid y) \)  “What does a cat look like?”
Learns the distribution of each class

To generate realistic samples, a model must capture the structure of the data, not just a boundary.

Boundary vs Distribution

Discriminative  \( p(y \mid x) \)
Generative  \( p(x \mid y) \)

A boundary is enough to classify; only a distribution lets us generate.

Uses of Generative Models

Evaluate
\( p_\theta(x) \)
Likelihood

How likely is this observation?

e.g. anomaly detection

Sample
\( \tilde{x} \sim p_\theta(x) \)
Generation

Generate a new observation

e.g. new images, text, audio

Condition
\( \tilde{x} \sim p_\theta(x \mid c) \)
Control

Generate data given a condition

e.g. text-to-image, editing

A generative model learns \( p(x) \), so it can evaluate, create and control data.

3.
Learning & Sampling

The Data Distribution

We have a dataset, but not the distribution that produced it:

$$ \mathcal{D} = \{x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_N\}, \qquad x_i \sim p_{\text{data}}(x) $$
What we have
\( \mathcal{D} \)

A finite set of samples

What we want
\( \boxed{p_\theta(x) \approx p_{\text{data}}(x)} \)

A model of the distribution itself

We observe samples from the data distribution, not the distribution itself.

Learning a Generative Model

Given a model family \( p_\theta(x) \) with parameters \( \theta\) and a dataset \( \mathcal{D} \), which \( \theta \) should we choose?

\( \theta^* = \arg\max_\theta \; \text{?} \)
Likelihood
\( \theta^* = \arg\max_\theta \prod_{i=1}^{N} p_\theta(x_i) \)

Probability of the whole dataset

Log-likelihood
\( \theta^* = \arg\max_\theta \sum_{i=1}^{N} \log p_\theta(x_i) \)

Same optimum, easier to compute

Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE): choose the \( \theta \) under which the observed data is most probable.

What Does Likelihood Mean?

Does the model assign high probability to the data we observe?

Model A
high likelihood
Model B
low likelihood

The model that puts its mass where the data lies has higher likelihood.

Learning and sampling are different problems: each model family solves them in its own way.

Why Is Sampling Difficult?

Learning a distribution is not the same as sampling from it.

Data
High-dimensional and structured: a \( 256 \times 256 \times 3 \) image has about 200,000 dimensions
Goal
Samples should be  Realistic Diverse Consistent
Method
Sampling depends strongly on how the distribution is represented

Different generative models use different strategies for sampling.

4.
Why Is It Hard?

The Root Problem

We only observe samples, not the distribution itself.

The Nature of the Data

Even with enough samples, the data itself is hard to model:

📐 High-dimensional

images, audio, video, text

🏔️ Multimodal

complex distributions, many separate modes

🔗 Dependencies

strong, across dimensions and scales

Competing Objectives

What we want from a model does not always point in the same direction:

Quality ↔ Diversity
Statistical fit ↔ Perceived sample quality

A model that assigns high probability to the observed data does not necessarily produce the samples humans prefer.

Practical Constraints

Even a good model must be usable in practice:

⏱️ Sampling speed

some models need many steps per sample

⚡ Compute

large models, memory and long training runs

📏 Evaluation

no single metric captures sample quality

Why So Many Model Families?

Each one makes different modelling and computational trade-offs.
Tractable likelihood ↔ Flexible generation
Training stability ↔ Model expressiveness
Sample quality ↔ Diversity
Generation quality ↔ Sampling speed
Explicit density modelling ↔ Implicit generation

Different model families make different choices to address these challenges.

Summary

\( x_i \sim p_{\text{data}}(x) \;\longrightarrow\; p_\theta(x) \approx p_{\text{data}}(x) \;\longrightarrow\; \tilde{x} \sim p_\theta(x) \)
  • A generative model learns the data distribution from samples, then draws new data from it
  • Unlike \( p(y \mid x) \), modelling \( p(x) \), \( p(x, y) \) or \( p(x \mid c) \) lets us evaluate, create and control data
  • Learning (e.g. MLE) and sampling are different problems, both hard for high-dimensional, multimodal data
  • Every model family is a different trade-off in how to represent, learn and sample
