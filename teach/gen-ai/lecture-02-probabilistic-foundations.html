1.
Random Variables & Distributions

Random Variables

A random variable assigns a numerical value to each outcome of a random experiment.
Discrete
\( x \in \{1, \dots, K\} \)

countably many values

a token, a class label

Continuous
\( x \in \mathbb{R} \)

values in a continuum

a pixel intensity, an audio amplitude

An image is a single high-dimensional random vector \( x \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times C} \).

Probability Distributions

Discrete: mass function
\( p(x) \ge 0, \;\; \sum_x p(x) = 1 \)

\( p(x) \) is a probability

Continuous: density
\( p(x) \ge 0, \;\; \int p(x)\, dx = 1 \)

\( p(x)\,dx \) is a probability

A density can exceed 1: only its integral must equal 1.

Common Distributions

Categorical
\( p(x = k) = \pi_k \)

one of \( K \) discrete outcomes

next-token prediction

Gaussian
\( \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2) \)

bell-shaped, set by mean and variance

VAE prior, diffusion noise

Uniform
\( \mathcal{U}(a, b) \)

every value equally likely

the raw source of randomness

Generative models turn samples from these simple distributions into complex data.

The Gaussian

$$ \mathcal{N}(x; \mu, \sigma^2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) $$
Two parameters
\( \mu, \; \sigma^2 \)

mean and variance describe it fully

Multivariate
\( \mathcal{N}(\mu, \Sigma) \)

a mean vector and a covariance matrix

Easy to sample
\( x = \mu + \sigma \epsilon \)

shift and scale \( \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1) \)

It appears as the prior in VAEs and as the noise in diffusion models.

2.
Joint, Marginal & Conditional

Joint, Marginal, Conditional

Joint
\( p(x, y) \)

both variables together

Marginal
\( p(x) = \int p(x, y)\, dy \)

integrate out what we do not need

Conditional
\( p(y \mid x) = \dfrac{p(x, y)}{p(x)} \)

\( y \) when \( x \) is known

The Product Rule

$$ p(x, y) = p(y \mid x)\, p(x) = p(x \mid y)\, p(y) $$
Repeat it
the chain rule

factorise many variables one at a time

↔
Rearrange it
Bayes rule

turn \( p(x \mid y) \) into \( p(y \mid x) \)

Both key rules of this section follow from this one identity.

The Chain Rule

$$ p(x_1, \ldots, x_D) = \prod_{i=1}^{D} p(x_i \mid x_{\lt i}) $$
\( p(x_1) \)
→
\( p(x_2 \mid x_1) \)
→
\( p(x_3 \mid x_1, x_2) \)
→
\( \cdots \)

One hard \( D \)-dimensional problem becomes \( D \) one-dimensional ones: the basis of autoregressive models (Lectures 04–05).

Bayes Rule

$$ \underbrace{p(z \mid x)}_{\text{posterior}} = \frac{\overbrace{p(x \mid z)}^{\text{likelihood}} \; \overbrace{p(z)}^{\text{prior}}}{\underbrace{p(x)}_{\text{evidence}}} $$
Prior
\( p(z) \)

belief about \( z \) before seeing data

Likelihood
\( p(x \mid z) \)

how \( z \) generates \( x \)

Evidence
\( p(x) \)

\( \int p(x \mid z) p(z)\, dz \): usually intractable

Lecture 03 shows how variational inference sidesteps the intractable evidence.

Independence

Independent
\( p(x, y) = p(x)\, p(y) \)

knowing \( x \) says nothing about \( y \)

Conditionally independent
\( p(x, y \mid z) = p(x \mid z)\, p(y \mid z) \)

independent once \( z \) is known

Latent-variable models assume the pixels become independent given the latent \( z \).

3.
Expectation & Sampling

Expectation

The average value of a function \( f \) under a distribution \( p \):

$$ \mathbb{E}_{p(x)}[f(x)] = \sum_x p(x) f(x) \quad \text{or} \quad \int p(x) f(x)\, dx $$
Training loss
\( \mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}}[-\log p_\theta(x)] \)

average over the data

Latent objectives
\( \mathbb{E}_{q(z \mid x)}[\log p(x \mid z)] \)

average over the latent

Nearly every training objective in this course is an expectation.

Monte Carlo Estimation

$$ \mathbb{E}_{p(x)}[f(x)] \approx \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} f(x_i), \qquad x_i \sim p(x) $$

Unbiased, with error \( \propto 1/\sqrt{N} \): this is why a minibatch can stand in for the whole dataset.

Ancestral Sampling

For a factorised model \( p(x, z) = p(z)\, p(x \mid z) \), sample in the order of the factorisation:

\( z \sim p(z) \)
→
\( x \sim p(x \mid z) \)
→
new sample \( x \)
1. Latent
draw from the prior

e.g. Gaussian noise

2. Data
decode / transform

e.g. a neural network

This is how a VAE or a GAN produces an image: draw \( z \), then map it to \( x \).

Change of Variables

$$ x = f(z) \;\; \text{invertible} \quad \Rightarrow \quad p_x(x) = p_z(z) \left| \det \frac{\partial z}{\partial x} \right| $$

The Jacobian term tracks how the map stretches space: the foundation of normalizing flows (Lecture 08).

4.
Entropy & KL Divergence

Entropy

$$ H(p) = -\mathbb{E}_{p(x)}[\log p(x)] $$
Fair coin
\( H = 1 \) bit

maximal uncertainty

Biased coin (90/10)
\( H \approx 0.47 \) bits

fairly predictable

Always heads
\( H = 0 \)

no uncertainty

Entropy measures uncertainty: the average surprise \( -\log p(x) \).

Cross-Entropy

$$ H(p, q) = -\mathbb{E}_{p(x)}[\log q(x)] $$
Meaning

the average surprise when data come from \( p \) but we predict with a model \( q \)

In practice

the standard loss for classifiers and language models

\( H(p, q) \ge H(p) \): using the wrong model always costs extra.

KL Divergence

$$ D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \,\|\, q) = \mathbb{E}_{p(x)}\!\left[\log \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right] $$
Non-negative
\( D_{\mathrm{KL}} \ge 0 \)

the extra cost is never negative

Zero at a match
\( = 0 \) iff \( p = q \)

it measures a gap

Not symmetric
\( D(p \| q) \ne D(q \| p) \)

so it is not a distance

KL is the core regulariser in the VAE objective.

Cross-Entropy and KL

$$ H(p, q) = H(p) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \,\|\, q) $$
Cross-entropy
\( H(p, q) \)

what we minimise

Entropy
\( H(p) \)

fixed by the data

KL gap
\( D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \| q) \)

what actually changes

Minimising cross-entropy is the same as minimising KL.

Forward vs Reverse KL

Fit a single Gaussian \( q \) to a two-mode target \( p \):

Intuition for failure modes: over-spread, blurry samples (forward) vs. samples that miss modes (reverse).

5.
Maximum Likelihood

Likelihood Estimation

Choose the parameters that make the observed data most probable:

$$ \theta^\star = \arg\max_\theta \prod_{i=1}^{N} p_\theta(x_i) = \arg\max_\theta \sum_{i=1}^{N} \log p_\theta(x_i) $$
Same optimum

\( \log \) is increasing, so the maximiser does not change

Numerically stable

products of many small numbers underflow; sums do not

MLE is the default training principle for most generative models.

MLE Minimises KL

$$ D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\text{data}} \| p_\theta) = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}}[\log p_{\text{data}}(x)]}_{\text{constant in } \theta} - \mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}}[\log p_\theta(x)] $$
\( \max_\theta \mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}}[\log p_\theta(x)] \)
→
\( \min_\theta D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\text{data}} \| p_\theta) \)

Fitting by likelihood is matching the data distribution: Lecture 01's goal \( p_\theta \approx p_{\text{data}} \) as a concrete loss.

Jensen's Inequality

Statement
\( \log \mathbb{E}[X] \ge \mathbb{E}[\log X] \)
Reason
\( \log \) is concave
Use
an intractable \( \log \int \) gets a tractable lower bound

This is exactly the step that produces the ELBO in Lecture 03.

Why This Matters

Chain rule
→ autoregressive models (Lectures 04–05)
Bayes + Jensen
→ the ELBO and VAEs (Lectures 03, 06–07)
Change of variables
→ normalizing flows (Lecture 08)
Divergences
→ GAN objectives (Lectures 09–10)
Gaussian noise
→ diffusion models (Lectures 12–14)

Every tool in this lecture reappears as the core of a model family.

Summary

$$ \max_\theta \; \mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}}[\log p_\theta(x)] \;\; \Longleftrightarrow \;\; \min_\theta \; D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\text{data}} \,\|\, p_\theta) $$
  • A distribution is a mass or density function that sums or integrates to one
  • The chain rule and Bayes rule both come from the product rule
  • Monte Carlo turns intractable expectations into sample averages
  • KL divergence measures the gap between distributions; MLE minimises it
