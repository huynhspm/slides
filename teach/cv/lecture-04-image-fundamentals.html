1.
Digital Image Representation

What is a Digital Image?

Definition: A digital image is a 2D function $f(x, y)$, where $(x, y)$ are spatial coordinates and $f$ is the intensity at that point — a value called a pixel.

  • Pixel values are nonnegative and finite: $0 \le f(x,y) < \infty$
  • Their physical meaning depends on the imaging source (light, X-ray, ultrasound…)
  • Also called a picture element, image element, or pel

Illumination & Reflectance

Image intensity is the product of two components:

$$f(x, y) = i(x, y) \cdot r(x, y)$$

Illumination $i(x,y)$

Light hitting the scene
$0 \le i < \infty$

Reflectance $r(x,y)$

Fraction reflected
$0 \le r \le 1$

Key Idea: What we see combines how the scene is lit with how its surfaces reflect light.

An Image as a Matrix

An $M \times N$ image is stored as a numerical array:

$$f = \begin{bmatrix} f(0,0) & \cdots & f(0,N\!-\!1) \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ f(M\!-\!1,0) & \cdots & f(M\!-\!1,N\!-\!1) \end{bmatrix}$$

  • Origin at top-left: $f(0,0)$
  • $x$ = row index (down), $y$ = column index (right)
  • $M, N$ are positive integers

2.
Sampling & Quantization

Digitizing a Continuous Image

A sensor gives a continuous function $f(x,y)$. Turning it into a digital image takes two steps:

🔲
Sampling

Digitize the coordinates $(x, y)$ — take values on a regular grid.

📊
Quantization

Digitize the amplitude — round each sample to a discrete level.

[Figure placeholder]
Description: A scan line of a continuous image sampled at regular positions and then quantized to discrete levels.
Image prompt: The Gonzalez Fig 2.16 style: continuous image → sampled dots along a scan line → quantized values on a gray-level scale.

Sampling Rate & Aliasing

Nyquist rule: To capture a signal without loss, sample at least twice its highest frequency.

  • Too coarse a grid → high frequencies fold into low ones: aliasing (moiré patterns)
  • Remedy: low-pass filter (blur) before sampling
  • Finer sampling → more pixels → higher spatial resolution
[Figure placeholder]
Description: An under-sampled striped/textured image showing moiré, vs. an anti-aliased version.
Image prompt: Two versions of a fine-striped pattern — one with strong moiré aliasing, one pre-blurred and cleanly sampled.

Two Kinds of Resolution

Spatial Resolution

The smallest discernible detail — number of pixels ($M \times N$). Measured in dpi or line pairs per unit distance.

Intensity Resolution

Number of gray levels $L = 2^k$. A conventional grayscale image uses $k=8 \Rightarrow L = 256$ levels.

[Figure placeholder]
Description: The same image shown at decreasing spatial resolution (e.g. 930→72 dpi), becoming blockier.
Image prompt: A 2×2 grid of the same photo at 930, 300, 150, and 72 dpi showing progressive loss of fine detail.

False Contouring

Reducing the number of intensity levels causes false contouring — fine ridge-like edges appear in smooth regions.

  • 128 & 64 levels: nearly identical to 256
  • 32 levels: faint ridges appear
  • 16 or fewer: clearly objectionable
[Figure placeholder]
Description: The same image quantized to 256, 32, 16, and 8 levels, showing false contours emerging.
Image prompt: A grid of the same smooth-gradient image at 256, 32, 16, and 8 intensity levels showing banding/contour artifacts.

How Many Levels Are Enough?

  • Digital images are typically quantized to 256 gray levels (8-bit)
  • Contouring is most visible on smooth ramps and gradients
  • Contrast ratio = ratio of highest to lowest intensity in the image
  • Spatial and intensity resolution trade off against storage and quality

Key Idea: Choosing $M$, $N$, and $L$ balances visual quality against the amount of data to store and process.

3.
Pixel Relationships

Neighbors of a Pixel

$N_4(p)$

The 4 horizontal & vertical neighbors.

$N_D(p)$

The 4 diagonal neighbors.

$N_8(p)$

All 8 surrounding pixels ($N_4 \cup N_D$).

[Figure placeholder]
Description: A 3×3 grid around a center pixel $p$, highlighting the 4-, diagonal, and 8-neighborhoods.
Image prompt: Three small 3×3 grids showing $N_4$ (plus shape), $N_D$ (X shape), and $N_8$ (all eight cells) around a central pixel.

Adjacency & Connectivity

  • Two pixels are adjacent if they are neighbors and their values satisfy a similarity criterion (e.g. same intensity set $V$)
  • 4-adjacency: $q \in N_4(p)$  ·  8-adjacency: $q \in N_8(p)$
  • m-adjacency (mixed): removes the ambiguity of multiple 8-connected paths
  • A connected component is a maximal set of mutually connected pixels — the basis of region labeling

Key Idea: Connectivity defines what counts as one "object" — essential for segmentation and morphology.

Distance Measures

Euclidean   $D_e(p,q) = \sqrt{(x_1\!-\!x_2)^2 + (y_1\!-\!y_2)^2}$
City-block ($D_4$)   $D_4(p,q) = |x_1\!-\!x_2| + |y_1\!-\!y_2|$
Chessboard ($D_8$)   $D_8(p,q) = \max\big(|x_1\!-\!x_2|,\, |y_1\!-\!y_2|\big)$

Key Idea: $D_4$ and $D_8$ correspond to moving between pixels along 4- and 8-connected paths.

4.
Basic Operations on Images

Arithmetic Operations

Applied element-wise between images of the same size:

Addition

Averaging to reduce noise; HDR imaging.

Subtraction

Change detection; background subtraction.

Multiplication

ROI masking (multiply by a 0/1 mask).

Division

Shading correction $f = g / h$.

Example: Averaging & Subtraction

Averaging $N$ noisy frames of a static scene reduces random noise by a factor of $\sqrt{N}$.

Subtraction of consecutive video frames isolates moving objects from a static background.

Logical & Set Operations

  • Logical operations (AND, OR, NOT, XOR) work between corresponding pixels of same-size binary images
  • Set operations treat foreground pixels as sets of coordinates (union, intersection, complement)
  • Foundation for masking and the morphological operations studied later

Summary

  • A digital image is $f(x,y) = i(x,y)\,r(x,y)$, stored as an $M \times N$ matrix of pixels.
  • Spatial and intensity resolution set detail and tonal quality; too few levels cause false contouring.
  • Pixels relate through neighborhoods, adjacency/connectivity, and distance measures.
  • Arithmetic, logical, and set operations are the building blocks of image processing.
  • These fundamentals prepare us for enhancement, filtering, and morphology.
