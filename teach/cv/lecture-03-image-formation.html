1.
Image Acquisition

What is an Image?

Image formation: The process by which light from a 3D scene is projected onto a 2D sensor to produce an image.

  • An image records the light reflected (or emitted) by surfaces in a scene
  • It depends on the scene geometry, the lighting, the surface properties, and the camera
  • Understanding formation lets us invert it — to recover scene information from images

The Acquisition Pipeline

1

Illumination — an energy source lights the scene

2

Reflection — surfaces reflect energy toward the camera

3

Optics — a lens focuses the light onto the image plane

4

Sensing — a sensor converts light into electrical signals

5

Digitization — signals are converted to a digital image (next lecture)

Sensing Color & Space

Color sensing

A color filter array (e.g. the Bayer pattern) lets each sensor site record R, G, or B; the full color is interpolated (demosaicing).

Spatial sensing

A 2D grid of sensing elements samples the scene across space, producing the pixel grid.

2.
The Pinhole Camera Model

The Pinhole Camera

The simplest camera: a tiny hole lets a single ray from each scene point pass to the image plane, forming an inverted image.

  • No lens — infinite depth of field
  • Small hole → sharp but dim
  • Large hole → bright but blurry

Perspective Projection

A 3D point $(X, Y, Z)$ projects to the image plane at focal length $f$:

$$x = f\frac{X}{Z}, \qquad y = f\frac{Y}{Z}$$

  • Division by $Z$ makes farther objects appear smaller
  • Parallel lines converge to a vanishing point
  • This is why perspective distorts sizes and angles

Projection in Homogeneous Form

Using homogeneous coordinates, projection is linear:

$$Z\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} f & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & f & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} X \\ Y \\ Z \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

Key Idea: Homogeneous coordinates turn the nonlinear perspective divide into a matrix multiplication — convenient for calibration and geometry.

3.
Cameras with Lenses

Why Lenses?

  • A pinhole gathers little light — real cameras use a lens with a larger aperture
  • A lens focuses many rays from a point back to a single image point — bright and sharp
  • Governed by the thin-lens equation:

$$\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{z_o} + \frac{1}{z_i}$$

$f$ = focal length, $z_o$ = object distance, $z_i$ = image distance

Focal Length, Aperture & Focus

Focal length $f$

Sets the field of view — long $f$ zooms in (narrow FOV), short $f$ is wide-angle.

Aperture

Controls light and depth of field — small aperture = more in focus.

Focus

Only objects at the right distance are sharp; others blur (the "circle of confusion").

Lens Effects & Distortion

Real lenses deviate from the ideal model:

  • Radial distortion — straight lines bow (barrel / pincushion)
  • Vignetting — darker corners
  • Chromatic aberration — color fringing at edges

4.
Camera Parameters & Representations

Intrinsic Parameters

Intrinsics map camera-space points to pixel coordinates, packed in the calibration matrix $K$:

$$K = \begin{bmatrix} f_x & s & c_x \\ 0 & f_y & c_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

  • $f_x, f_y$ — focal length in pixels
  • $c_x, c_y$ — principal point (image center)
  • $s$ — skew (usually 0)

Extrinsic Parameters

Extrinsics place the camera in the world — a rotation $R$ and translation $t$:

$$\tilde{x} = K\,[\,R \mid t\,]\,\tilde{X}$$

Key Idea: The full camera model $P = K[R \mid t]$ maps 3D world points to 2D image points — finding it is called camera calibration.

Ways to Represent a Scene

2D image

A grid of pixel intensities / colors.

3D image / depth

Adds a depth value per pixel (RGB-D).

Point cloud

A set of 3D points $(X, Y, Z)$.

Mesh

Points connected into surfaces (triangles).

Summary

  • Image formation projects a lit 3D scene onto a 2D sensor through optics.
  • The pinhole model gives perspective projection $x = fX/Z$, $y = fY/Z$.
  • Real cameras use lenses (focal length, aperture, focus) and sensors (CCD/CMOS), with lens distortion.
  • The camera model $P = K[R\mid t]$ combines intrinsics and extrinsics.
  • Scenes can be represented as 2D images, depth, point clouds, or meshes.
