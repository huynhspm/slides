1.
Light & the Visible Spectrum

Why Start with the Eye?

Motivation: Images are ultimately made for — or inspired by — the human visual system. Understanding how we perceive light guides how we capture, process, and evaluate images.

  • Perception defines what "good quality" means for an image
  • Many algorithms mimic the eye (e.g. gamma correction, color spaces)
  • Perceptual limits tell us what information we can safely discard (e.g. in compression)

Light & the Electromagnetic Spectrum

  • Light is electromagnetic radiation, described by wavelength $\lambda$ or as photons
  • The visible spectrum spans only $\approx 0.43\,\mu m$ to $0.79\,\mu m$ — a tiny slice of the full spectrum
  • Split into six regions: violet, blue, green, yellow, orange, red

Color, Intensity & Brightness

Radiance

Total energy from a source, in watts (W).

Luminance

Light intensity perceived from the source surface.

Brightness

Subjective perception of light by the human eye.

Key Idea: An infrared source has high radiance but near-zero brightness — the eye simply cannot perceive it. Monochromatic light has a single attribute: intensity (a.k.a. gray level).

Structure of the Human Eye

Anatomy of the Eye

  • Cornea & sclera — tough transparent outer cover
  • Choroid — blood vessels; dark layer limiting stray light
  • Iris & pupil — control light entering (2–8 mm)
  • Lens — flexible; focuses light, absorbs UV/IR
  • Retina — inner surface where the image forms

Photoreceptors: Rods & Cones

Cones Rods
6–7 million, in the central fovea 75–150 million, spread across the retina
Sensitive to color & fine detail Sensitive to dim light, no color
Photopic (bright / day) vision Scotopic (dim / night) vision

In dim light only rods fire — a colorful flower is seen in shades of gray.

Color Vision: Three Cone Types

S cones

400–500 nm
violet → blue

M cones

500–600 nm
green

L cones

600–700 nm
red

Key Idea: Combining the responses of S, M, and L cones lets us perceive the full range of colors — the basis of trichromatic vision.

Image Formation & Focusing

  • Unlike a rigid glass lens, the eye's lens is flexible
  • Ciliary muscles flatten the lens for distant objects, thicken it for near objects — called accommodation
  • The lens-to-fovea distance varies from ~17 mm (far) to ~14 mm (near)

3.
Brightness Perception

Brightness Adaptation

  • The eye perceives an enormous range of intensities — from dim threshold to glare
  • But not all at once: it adapts to a current adaptation level $B_a$
  • Subjective brightness is a logarithmic function of light intensity
  • This is why, after the lights go out, the eye needs time to adjust before it can see
[Figure placeholder]

Image prompt: A plot of subjective brightness against log light intensity with the long overall adaptation range and a short branch curve passing through the adaptation level $B_a$.

Intensity Discrimination — Weber's Law

Experiment: On a uniform background of intensity $I$, we add $\Delta I$ to a central patch. The smallest $\Delta I_c$ the eye can detect defines the Weber ratio.

$$\text{Weber ratio} = \frac{\Delta I_c}{I}$$

Small ratio → good discrimination
Large ratio → poor discrimination

Discrimination Depends on Background

  • Discrimination is poor in dim light and improves as background brightness rises
  • Cones (photopic) discriminate better than rods (scotopic)
  • Practical meaning: the eye is more sensitive to relative than to absolute intensity changes

4.
Perceptual Phenomena

Perception ≠ Physical Intensity

Insight: Perceived brightness depends not only on an object's intensity, but also on its spatial context and edges.

Two famous demonstrations reveal this gap between the physical signal and what we see: Mach bands and simultaneous contrast.

Mach Bands

At the boundary between two uniform intensity regions, the eye perceives the edge as brighter on the light side and darker on the dark side — even though intensity is constant within each stripe.

This edge enhancement is caused by lateral inhibition in the retina.

Simultaneous Contrast

A region's perceived brightness depends on its surrounding background.

Identical gray squares appear lighter on a dark background and darker on a light background — though their physical intensity is the same.

Optical Illusions

The visual system fills in, completes, and sometimes invents information that is not physically present.

Illusions remind us that the eye is an active interpreter, not a passive camera.

Summary

  • Vision responds to the tiny visible band of the electromagnetic spectrum.
  • The eye focuses light onto the retina; cones give color/detail (photopic), rods give dim-light vision (scotopic).
  • Brightness perception is logarithmic and adaptive; Weber's law governs discrimination.
  • Mach bands and simultaneous contrast show perception depends on context, not just intensity.
  • These insights shape enhancement, color, and compression algorithms.
